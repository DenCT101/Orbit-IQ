Late blight is caused by the oomycete Phytophthora infestans. This disease is historically famous for causing the Irish Potato Famine (1845-1852). It remains the most destructive disease of potato and tomato worldwide, capable of destroying entire crops within days under favorable conditions.

SYMPTOMS AND IDENTIFICATION:
Leaf Symptoms: Water-soaked, pale green to dark green spots appear on leaf tips and edges, rapidly expanding into large, brown-black lesions. Under humid conditions, a white, downy growth of sporangia appears on the lower leaf surface at the margin of lesions. This sporulation distinguishes late blight from other leaf blights. Affected tissue dies rapidly, and a characteristic musty odor may be detected.

Stem Symptoms: Dark brown to black, water-soaked lesions appear on stems and petioles. Stem infections can girdle and kill branches or the entire plant.

Tuber Symptoms: Irregular, slightly depressed areas with brown to purple discoloration appear on the tuber surface. Cutting through infected tubers reveals a rusty-brown, granular rot extending from the surface inward. Secondary bacteria (Pectobacterium spp.) commonly invade, causing soft rot and foul odors in storage.

DISEASE CYCLE:
Phytophthora infestans overwinters in infected tubers (seed pieces, volunteer plants, cull piles). Under cool (50-70°F), wet conditions with high humidity (>90%), sporangia are produced on infected tissue and dispersed by wind over long distances (up to 30+ miles). Sporangia can directly infect or produce motile zoospores that swim in water films on leaf surfaces. The entire infection cycle (infection to sporulation) can be as short as 3-5 days, allowing explosive epidemic development.

TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT:
1. Certified Seed: Plant only certified, disease-free seed potatoes. Inspect seed carefully and reject lots with any signs of late blight.
2. Eliminate Inoculum Sources: Destroy volunteer potatoes, cull piles, and abandoned potato plots. These are the primary sources of initial inoculum each season.
3. Fungicide Programs: Apply protectant fungicides (chlorothalonil, mancozeb) preventatively on a 5-7 day schedule during periods of favorable weather. Systemic fungicides (metalaxyl/mefenoxam, fluopicolide, cymoxanil) provide excellent control but must be rotated to prevent resistance. Monitor for metalaxyl-resistant strains.
4. Late Blight Forecasting: Use disease prediction models (BLITECAST, BlightPro) based on temperature and humidity to time fungicide applications. Begin sprays when models indicate favorable conditions for disease development.
5. Resistant Varieties: Plant varieties with resistance to late blight where available. 'Defender', 'Elba', and 'Jacqueline Lee' have good resistance. Resistance should complement, not replace, fungicide programs.
6. Harvest Management: Kill vines 2-3 weeks before harvest to allow skin set and prevent tuber infection during harvest. Avoid harvesting in wet conditions. Dry tubers before storage.

New strains of P. infestans continue to emerge, some with resistance to metalaxyl (the most effective systemic fungicide). The US-23 clonal lineage is currently dominant in the United States. Genotype tracking through USAblight.org helps growers and researchers monitor strain distribution and adapt management recommendations.
