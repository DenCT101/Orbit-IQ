Two-spotted spider mite (Tetranychus urticae) is one of the most common arthropod pests of tomatoes and many other crops. Although not a disease, spider mite infestations can cause significant damage to tomato foliage and reduce yields.

SYMPTOMS AND IDENTIFICATION:
Early Symptoms: Fine, pale stippling (tiny yellow dots) on the upper leaf surface caused by mites feeding on the undersides. Leaves may appear slightly bronzed or faded.

Advanced Symptoms: As populations increase, leaves become yellow, then brown, and brittle. Fine webbing becomes visible on leaf undersides and between leaves. Severely infested leaves dry up and fall off. Plants can become completely defoliated in severe infestations.

Mite Identification: Two-spotted spider mites are tiny (1/50 inch), oval, and pale green to yellowish with two dark spots on the body. They are found primarily on leaf undersides. Use a hand lens or magnifying glass for identification. Shake leaves over white paper — moving specks are mites.

BIOLOGY:
Spider mites thrive in hot (80-90°F), dry conditions. Populations can double every 3-5 days under favorable conditions. Females lay 100+ eggs over their 2-4 week lifespan. Mites overwinter in soil debris, on weeds, and in protected areas. Broad-spectrum insecticide applications can exacerbate mite problems by killing natural enemies (predatory mites, lacewings, ladybugs).

TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT:
1. Cultural Controls: Maintain adequate irrigation — water-stressed plants are more susceptible. Control broadleaf weeds that serve as alternate hosts. Avoid excessive nitrogen, which promotes mite-favorable succulent growth.
2. Biological Control: Conserve and augment natural predators. Predatory mites (Phytoseiulus persimilis, Neoseiulus californicus) are highly effective biological control agents, especially in greenhouses. Avoid broad-spectrum insecticides that kill beneficial predators.
3. Miticides: When populations warrant treatment, use selective miticides (abamectin, bifenazate, spiromesifen) that preserve natural enemies. Horticultural oil (1-2%) and insecticidal soap provide effective control with minimal impact on beneficials. Thorough coverage of leaf undersides is essential.
4. Water Sprays: Strong water sprays can dislodge mites and disrupt webbing. Apply in early morning. Repeat every 3-5 days during active infestations.
5. Monitoring: Scout weekly by examining leaf undersides with a hand lens. Economic thresholds vary but generally action is warranted when 25-50% of leaves show mite feeding damage.

Spider mites are the most important arthropod pests in many cropping systems. Their rapid reproduction rate, ability to develop pesticide resistance, and tendency to outbreak following broad-spectrum insecticide applications make them particularly challenging to manage. Integrated approaches combining cultural controls, biological control, and selective miticides provide the most sustainable management.
