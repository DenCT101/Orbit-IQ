Bacterial spot of peach and nectarine is caused by the bacterium Xanthomonas arboricola pv. pruni. It is one of the most serious diseases of stone fruit in warm, humid climates, particularly in the southeastern United States.

SYMPTOMS AND IDENTIFICATION:
Leaf Symptoms: Small (1-3 mm), angular, water-soaked spots appear on leaves. Spots turn dark brown to purple and may be surrounded by a yellow halo. Lesions often concentrate near leaf tips and margins where moisture collects. Severely affected leaves develop a "shot-hole" appearance as dead tissue falls out. Premature defoliation is common in severe infections.

Fruit Symptoms: Small, brown, sunken spots appear on fruit, often with cracking. Spots can enlarge and deepen, creating entry points for secondary rot organisms. Severe fruit infections make peaches unmarketable. Symptoms are most common on fruit exposed to wind-driven rain.

Twig Symptoms: Dark, sunken cankers (spring cankers) develop on previous-year's growth. These cankers serve as overwintering sites for the bacteria and are the primary source of inoculum in spring.

DISEASE CYCLE:
Bacteria overwinter in twig cankers. In spring, rain splash disperses bacteria from cankers to developing leaves and fruit. Wind-driven rain is the primary means of spread. Warm temperatures (75-86°F) and frequent rainfall favor disease development. The bacterium enters through natural openings (stomata, lenticels) and wounds. Sandy soils and sites exposed to prevailing winds increase disease severity.

TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT:
1. Resistant Varieties: Plant varieties with good resistance to bacterial spot. Resistant varieties include 'Contender', 'Biscoe', 'Harrow Diamond', and 'Bounty'. Nectarines are generally more susceptible than peaches.
2. Copper Applications: Apply fixed copper sprays at leaf fall and before budbreak to reduce overwintering inoculum. Spring copper applications can cause phytotoxicity on leaves and fruit — use lower rates with caution.
3. Oxytetracycline: Antibiotic sprays (oxytetracycline — Mycoshield, Fireline) can provide suppression during the growing season. Apply every 5-7 days during wet weather. Most effective when combined with copper programs.
4. Cultural Practices: Avoid overhead irrigation. Maintain good air circulation through pruning. Reduce wind exposure with windbreaks. Maintain tree vigor through proper fertilization and irrigation.
5. Site Selection: Avoid planting in areas with heavy, poorly drained soils, low air drainage, or exposure to prevailing winds. These conditions favor disease development and severity.

Management of bacterial spot is challenging because no treatment can cure established infections. The integrated approach of resistant varieties, sanitation, copper applications, and favorable site selection provides the most reliable management. Growers should accept that some level of disease will occur even with the best management programs.
