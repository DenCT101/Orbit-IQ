Septoria leaf spot is caused by the fungus Septoria lycopersici. It is one of the most common and destructive foliage diseases of tomato, particularly in warm, humid areas with frequent rainfall.

SYMPTOMS AND IDENTIFICATION:
Small (1/16 to 1/4 inch), circular spots with dark brown margins and grayish-white centers appear on lower, older leaves. Tiny black dots (pycnidia) visible within the gray centers are the key diagnostic feature — they produce spores for secondary spread. Individual lesions may be small, but large numbers can coalesce, causing entire leaves to yellow, wither, and drop. The disease progresses rapidly from lower to upper leaves during warm, wet weather.

DISEASE CYCLE:
Septoria lycopersici overwinters on infected tomato debris, on solanaceous weeds (horsenettle, jimsonweed, nightshade), and possibly on seed. Spores (conidia) are produced in pycnidia and splashed by rain to new foliage. Infection requires 48+ hours of leaf wetness at temperatures of 60-80°F. Once established, the disease can cycle every 5-7 days under favorable conditions, causing rapid defoliation.

TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT:
1. Cultural Practices: Stake or cage plants to improve air circulation. Mulch around plants to prevent soil splash. Use drip irrigation. Remove lower leaves that show infection. Practice 3-year crop rotation with non-solanaceous crops.
2. Sanitation: Remove and destroy all tomato plant debris at the end of the season. Control solanaceous weeds in and around the garden.
3. Fungicide Applications: Apply chlorothalonil, mancozeb, or copper-based products on a 7-10 day schedule, starting at first symptom or when conditions favor disease. Systemic fungicides provide better control during heavy pressure.
4. Resistant Varieties: Few commercially available tomato varieties have strong Septoria resistance. Select varieties with the best available tolerance ratings.

Septoria leaf spot can cause severe defoliation within 2-3 weeks of the first symptoms appearing. Early detection and immediate fungicide application are critical. The disease is particularly damaging when it occurs before or during fruit ripening, as defoliated plants produce smaller, lower-quality fruit and are susceptible to sunscald.
