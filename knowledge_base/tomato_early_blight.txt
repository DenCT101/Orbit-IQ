Early blight of tomato is caused by the fungus Alternaria solani (and sometimes A. tomatophila). It is one of the most common and widespread diseases of tomato, occurring in virtually all tomato-growing regions.

SYMPTOMS AND IDENTIFICATION:
Characteristic dark brown to black lesions with concentric rings (target-board or bull's-eye pattern) appear first on older, lower leaves. A yellow halo often surrounds lesions. The disease progresses upward through the plant canopy. Stem lesions appear as dark, sunken areas often at or near the soil line. Fruit infections (via the stem scar) cause dark, leathery, sunken lesions at the stem end.

DISEASE CYCLE:
Alternaria solani overwinters in infected plant debris, on volunteer plants, and in the soil. Spores are dispersed by wind, rain, and overhead irrigation. Warm temperatures (75-85°F), alternating wet/dry conditions, and heavy dew favor disease. Older plants and stressed plants are most susceptible.

TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT:
1. Cultural Practices: Stake or cage plants to improve air circulation. Use mulch to prevent soil splash. Remove lower leaves as the plant grows. Practice crop rotation (3+ years).
2. Fungicides: Apply chlorothalonil, mancozeb, or copper on a 7-10 day schedule starting at transplant or first symptom. Systemic fungicides (azoxystrobin, difenoconazole) provide excellent control.
3. Resistant Varieties: Some varieties such as 'Mountain Magic', 'Defiant', and 'Jasper' have improved early blight resistance.
4. Sanitation: Remove and destroy all infected plant debris at season end. Clean stakes, cages, and equipment between seasons.

Early blight causes yield losses primarily through defoliation, which reduces the plant's ability to produce energy for fruit development. Maintaining healthy, green foliage throughout the season is the key to minimizing yield impact.
